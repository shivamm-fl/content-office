# Benchmark of 2026-10-18: what it taught (format and strategy only)

A research agent read nine public Instagram accounts in the system-design niche on 2026-10-18 (profile and Reels tabs, a few opened reels) and fetched four web pages. Everything here is **as read that day**, from public pages. Account-level numbers only: no comment text, no usernames, no handles (this repo is public). CLAUDE.md rule 15 applies: we take formats and strategy, never another account's scripts, wording, visuals, audio, mascot, colours, day list or topic order. The research report itself is not committed. Nothing here changes a rule; the consequences live in `docs-notes/series-style.md` and `.claude/skills/hook-writer`.

## What outperformed and what did not
- **Views stay flat per account whatever the caption asks.** On the cold account from the owner's feed (695 followers, 28 reels in about five days, about five a day) 20 of 28 reels sat between 1.2k and 2.1k views (median 1.5k), whatever the topic, cover or series tag, with no upward trend over six days.
- **Likes follow views.** Where likes could be read (about 12 reels on six accounts) the like rate was 14 to 27 per 1,000 views (1.4% to 2.7%) whoever made the reel; comments were 0.01% to 0.2% of views. On the account that asks viewers to comment a keyword, six of seven opened reels had 0 or 1 comments. The ask does not move engagement.
- **Inference, not measurement:** public pages show no retention, saves or sends. Because engagement scales with views, the lever we can pull is how many people are shown the reel and keep watching: the hook and watch-through, not caption tricks. Our own insights decide.
- **Beat their own account median by 3x or more:** 2 of 28 reels on the owner's-feed account, 3 of 16 on the closest analogue (a faceless animated series account, about 2.5k followers; up to 31x), 0 of 8 on a 203-follower Hindi account that still held about 3.6k views a reel (about 18x its followers). What those reels share: one short concrete question about an everyday moment or an interview confusion, naming an app or an event.
- **Steady rather than spiky:** a large account that tells dated incident stories with a link to the official report held a median of about 0.3x its followers over 20 reels (14k to 176k).
- **Did not work:** the lowest reel of 28 (195 views) had a long abstract English title; niche-utility and personal or off-topic posts were most of the bottom five; meme-only humour from a teaching account got reach without teaching; one account's old viral reels (up to 179k) did not lift later reels (1k to 6k); an account treated as an afterthought (3 reels, about 640 views each on 2.6k followers); more posts a day (see below).

## Series and named-column evidence
- **A number on the cover earns nothing by itself.** Numbered posts had a median of 1,400 views (n=10) against 1,545 unnumbered (n=7) on the owner's-feed account, where the number was a post counter and the topics jumped.
- **Named columns, weak evidence.** The only account with spikes at a small size runs about eight named columns, each with its own episode count; its two biggest reels sat in the two columns framed as a question. Confound: column, topic and hook change together, and it is one account, so we cannot tell which one did it. Treated as a hypothesis (see the owner decision in `series-style.md`).
- **Nobody among the nine offers a phased ladder with prerequisites and a progress bar.** The numbered accounts count posts. That gap is what the 150-day plan fills (claim limited to the nine accounts read).
- **Cadence.** The better multiples came from one to two reels a day; five a day on a cold account did not raise the baseline in six days (one account). We make one a day.
- **One-offs do not compound.** A ladder where each reel stands alone but belongs to a visible sequence is the fix the data points to.

## Language evidence and its confounds
- On the only account that mixes both, reels with Hinglish cover words had a median of about 1.6k (n=5) against about 1.4k for English covers (n=16): a 12% gap inside the noise, carried by one winner. Its captions are Hinglish throughout.
- Views per follower: the small Hindi or Hinglish accounts ran about 1x to 18x, the large English accounts 0.1x to 0.9x. Confounds: size dilutes reach, account age, topic quality, and only three small accounts.
- The closest analogue's two biggest reels both had Hinglish words on the cover; its English-covered posts stayed within 2x of its median. Same confounds, n=1.
- Hypothesis, untested: English technical depth draws corrections and follow-up questions, Hinglish humour draws short emoji comments. Precise terms stay in English.
- Market figure (fetched, 2020, YouTube viewing in India): Hindi 54% against English 16%. Six years old and one platform.
- **Verdict: no reliable language effect.** The floor stays Hinglish voice with English terms (owner decision). A Hinglish word on covers or an English-voice A/B are owner experiments, not rules; cover rule 6 says clean English and no Hindi.

## Hook formulas seen (format only; ours are in the hook-writer skill)
Seven fit a faceless Hinglish system-design reel: an everyday moment with "how is that possible"; a shared pain ("they all fail together"); a weird hypothetical with "will it?"; two concepts that sound alike with a one-line verdict; a hard-number stake; an incident post-mortem with a date and a source; a benchmark of A against B. Each was seen on one to three accounts, so each is a hypothesis to test side by side. The winners were short, concrete, named an app or an event and read as one question; the weak one was a long abstract sentence. Not adopted: a recurring named character (another account's mascot device; our cover rule says no mascot), version or launch news (no news desk, and rule 1 needs a fetched page), interview humour as a hook, and a "Day N+1" teaser at the close (breaks the stand-alone rule).

## Avoid list
1. Long abstract English titles, and "reverse-engineer it from outside" meta titles.
2. Jargon chains with no decode (several acronyms in one sentence); a beginner on-ramp is open ground in the niche (the report's gap analysis, not a measured effect).
3. Five to seven reels a day on a cold account.
4. Unrelated series (coding challenges, news, personal wraps) in the same grid.
5. Counting on "comment KEYWORD" to produce comments.
6. Meme-only reels from a teaching account.
7. Treating Instagram as an afterthought (a handful of reels a year), or one-off viral reels with no ladder behind them.
8. Glowing or flickering headline styling; ours is flat and calm (`clean-reel`).
9. Loosely worded claims: technical viewers correct them. Say "typically" or "one common design" and keep a source line.
10. Chasing a news spike with four variants in one day: only the first of four broke out (one data point).

## Limits of the data
- One afternoon, about 28 page loads. Reel length and audio were never seen; stated lengths (30 to 60 s) are what creators say in bios and captions. Language was judged from covers and captions.
- Saves and sends are invisible on public pages; likes and comments were read on about a dozen reels only; some reels were hours old and still climbing.
- Tiny samples, one account carries most of the numbers, accounts differ in age and size, no causal claims. The flat 1.5k on the owner's-feed account may be a fixed test audience per reel, not a ceiling.
- **Not used because the report marks them unverified (search snippets, never fetched):** third-party claims about which signals Instagram ranks (watch time, sends, saves), an Instagram "Series" feature, a competitor engagement benchmark for carousels against reels, and third-party YouTube channel statistics. One platform statement was fetched: Instagram's creator page says reels up to three minutes can reach non-followers; our 30 to 90 s rule is unaffected.
- First numbers of our own replace this note: after the first 30 days compare hook types by skip rate and watch-through in `state/learnings.md`.
